# Evidence for ux-psychology

The finding and sources behind each entry in `SKILL.md`, in the same order. Open this when a review finding needs backing, or when someone asks why a rule exists. It is not needed for designing. Full references are in `sources.md`.

## 1. Seeing: how the eye organises a screen

### Grouping by nearness and likeness
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: Elements that sit close together, or share colour, shape or size, are perceived as belonging together before any reading happens.
- Sources: Wertheimer 1923; Wagemans et al. 2012

### Grouping by enclosure and connection
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: A shared boundary or a connecting line groups elements, and can override nearness and likeness.
- Sources: Palmer 1992; Palmer & Rock 1994

### Simplicity and figure-ground
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: People resolve what they see into the simplest, most regular structure available, and separate a figure from its background.
- Sources: Koffka 1935; Wagemans et al. 2012

### Visual pop-out and distinctiveness
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: An item that differs from its neighbours on a basic feature (colour, size, orientation) is found without effort. Separately, an item that differs from the rest of a set is better remembered, and this does not require it to be visually striking. Both depend on the rest being uniform.
- Sources: Treisman & Gelade 1980; von Restorff 1933; Hunt 1995

### Processing fluency
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: Things that are easy to perceive and read are judged more positively and as more trustworthy.
- Sources: Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004; Alter & Oppenheimer 2009

### First impressions and the beauty halo
- Grade: Moderate, direction debated
- Finding: Visual appeal is judged within a fraction of a second. Early studies found that more attractive designs were rated as more usable, before and after use. A later experiment found no such effect, and found instead that poor usability lowered perceived beauty.
- Sources: Kurosu & Kashimura 1995; Tractinsky, Katz & Ikar 2000; Lindgaard et al. 2006; Tuch et al. 2012

## 2. Attending: what gets noticed

### Goal-directed attention and blindness to the unexpected
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: People focused on a task routinely fail to see clearly visible things unrelated to it, and miss changes to things they are not attending to.
- Sources: Simons & Chabris 1999; Rensink, O'Regan & Clark 1997

### Overlooking banner-like areas
- Grade: Moderate, original report not peer-reviewed
- Finding: People searching a page for information overlooked large, colourful banner-style links more often than plain text links, even when the banner held what they wanted.
- Sources: Benway & Lane 1998

### Habituation to repeated alerts
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: In a lab study, warnings that interrupted the task were heeded by most people and a passive one by almost no one. Field data show full-page browser warnings are mostly heeded, but the warnings people see most often are clicked through faster and more often.
- Sources: Egelman, Cranor & Hong 2008; Akhawe & Felt 2013

### Split attention
- Grade: Strong in instructional settings
- Finding: When related information is physically separated, such as a diagram and the text explaining it, people spend effort mentally joining it.
- Sources: Chandler & Sweller 1992

## 3. Remembering: what people can hold in mind

### Working memory is small
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: People hold only a few items in mind at once. Miller's "seven, plus or minus two" is widely misapplied; later work puts the limit nearer four chunks.
- Sources: Miller 1956; Cowan 2001

### Chunking
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: Grouping items into meaningful units increases how much can be taken in and retained.
- Sources: Miller 1956; Chase & Simon 1973

### Recognition memory is strong
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: After seeing hundreds of words, sentences or pictures once, people could pick out the ones they had seen with very high accuracy. Interfaces that let people choose from what is shown draw on this ability; those that demand unprompted recall do not.
- Sources: Shepard 1967

### Position in a list
- Grade: Strong for memory, Moderate for interfaces
- Finding: First and last items in a series are remembered best, and links in first position attract the most clicks, with a smaller lift for the last.
- Sources: Murdock 1962; Murphy, Hofacker & Mizerski 2006

### Cognitive load
- Grade: Strong in learning research
- Finding: Working memory used up by one demand is unavailable for another. The original work showed effortful problem-solving leaving little capacity for learning; later work showed poorly arranged material adding to the load.
- Sources: Sweller 1988; Chandler & Sweller 1992

### Unfinished tasks
- Grade: Contested
- Finding: The claim that interrupted tasks are remembered better than completed ones has not replicated reliably. Showing unfinished work is still good practice, for plainer reasons: it supports recognition and makes resuming easy.
- Sources: Zeigarnik 1927; MacLeod 2020

## 4. Deciding: how choices get made

### Choice reaction time
- Grade: Strong in the lab, Moderate for menus
- Finding: Time to choose among familiar alternatives rises with the logarithm of their number. For unfamiliar lists, people search item by item, which is slower and roughly linear.
- Sources: Hick 1952; Hyman 1953; Cockburn, Gutwin & Greenberg 2007

### Too many options
- Grade: Contested
- Finding: Large assortments sometimes reduce choosing and satisfaction, but a meta-analysis found an average effect near zero. It appears mainly when options are hard to compare, preferences are unclear, or time is short.
- Sources: Iyengar & Lepper 2000; Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd 2010; Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman 2015

### Defaults
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: People are substantially more likely to end up with the preselected option, though the size of the effect varies widely by context. A default is therefore a decision made on the user's behalf.
- Sources: Johnson & Goldstein 2003; Samuelson & Zeckhauser 1988; Jachimowicz et al. 2019

### Anchoring and framing
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: The first number seen pulls later judgements toward it, and the same facts presented as gains or losses lead to different choices.
- Sources: Tversky & Kahneman 1974; Tversky & Kahneman 1981

### Loss aversion
- Grade: Strong, size debated
- Finding: Losses weigh more heavily than equal gains.
- Sources: Kahneman & Tversky 1979

### Context effects between options
- Grade: Moderate, attraction effect contested
- Finding: Adding an option can shift preference among the others, toward a middle option or toward one that clearly beats a similar neighbour. The second effect often fails with realistic, non-numeric options.
- Sources: Huber, Payne & Puto 1982; Simonson 1989; Frederick, Lee & Baskin 2014

### Following others
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: Telling people what others in the same situation had done changed behaviour more than a standard appeal, most of all when the comparison group was closely matched to them.
- Sources: Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius 2008

## 5. Acting: pointing, tapping and making mistakes

### Pointing time
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: Time to reach a target grows with distance and shrinks with target size. For thumbs on touchscreens, one study recommended at least 9.2 mm for single taps and 9.6 mm for sequences of taps, close to Android's 48 dp guidance.
- Sources: Fitts 1954; MacKenzie 1992; Parhi, Karlson & Bederson 2006

### Steering through narrow paths
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: Moving a pointer along a constrained path takes longer as the path gets longer and narrower.
- Sources: Accot & Zhai 1997

### Compatibility between controls and effects
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: Responses are faster and more accurate when the layout of controls matches the layout of what they affect.
- Sources: Fitts & Seeger 1953

### Affordances and signifiers
- Grade: Framework
- Finding: People act on what an object appears to offer. In screens this comes from visible cues that signal what can be pressed, dragged or edited.
- Sources: Gibson 1979; Norman 1988; Norman 2013

### Slips and mistakes
- Grade: Framework with strong empirical basis
- Finding: Norman classified slips, where the action does not match the intention. Reason separated slips and memory lapses from mistakes, where the plan itself is wrong. They need different remedies.
- Sources: Norman 1981; Reason 1990

## 6. Waiting: time and feedback

### Response-time thresholds
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: Acceptable delay depends on the kind of action. Miller argued for about a tenth of a second for feedback on a keypress and about two seconds for most conversational responses. Card and colleagues set out three bands: about 0.1 s for perceptual processing, about 1 s for an immediate response, and about 10 s for a unit of work. An IBM report argued for sub-second response on productivity grounds; it was not peer-reviewed. The exact thresholds quoted in design advice are rules of thumb, not measured limits.
- Sources: Miller 1968; Card, Robertson & Mackinlay 1991; Doherty & Thadani 1982

### How waits feel
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: Unexplained, uncertain and unoccupied waits feel longer. Progress indicators are preferred over none, and progress that accelerates toward the end feels faster.
- Sources: Maister 1985; Myers 1985; Harrison et al. 2007

## 7. Feeling: motivation and memory of the experience

### Progress toward a goal
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: Effort increases as a goal gets closer, and people given a head start are more likely to finish.
- Sources: Hull 1932; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006; Nunes & Drèze 2006

### Peaks and endings
- Grade: Strong for short episodes, weaker for long ones
- Finding: Remembered experience is dominated by the most intense moment and the final moment, more than by duration.
- Sources: Kahneman et al. 1993; Redelmeier & Kahneman 1996

### Absorption in a task
- Grade: Framework
- Finding: People describe deep engagement when challenge matches skill, goals are clear and feedback is immediate.
- Sources: Csikszentmihalyi 1990

### Autonomy and competence
- Grade: Strong as theory
- Finding: People are more motivated and satisfied when they feel in control and capable.
- Sources: Deci & Ryan 2000

## 8. Learning: expectations and first use

### Mental models
- Grade: Framework
- Finding: People reason with internal models of how things work. Norman observed that users' models of devices are incomplete, unstable and often wrong, and differ from the designer's.
- Sources: Johnson-Laird 1983; Norman 1983

### Practice and familiarity
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: Practised actions become faster along a predictable curve, and repeated exposure to something increases liking for it.
- Sources: Newell & Rosenbloom 1981; Zajonc 1968

### People start doing, not reading
- Grade: Moderate, observational
- Finding: New users begin acting immediately and skip instructions, even when reading would save time.
- Sources: Carroll & Rosson 1987

### Staged complexity
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: Beginners given a reduced interface that blocks advanced functions learned faster and spent far less time recovering from errors.
- Sources: Carroll & Carrithers 1984
