# Evidence for ux-writing

The finding and sources behind each entry in `SKILL.md`, in the same order. Open this when a review finding needs backing, or when someone asks why a rule exists. It is not needed for writing. Full references are in `sources.md`.

## 1. Being understood

### People scan before they read
- Grade: Moderate, industry study
- Finding: In web reading studies most participants scanned pages instead of reading word by word, and rewriting a page to be concise, scannable and neutral in tone more than doubled measured usability.
- Sources: Morkes & Nielsen 1997

### Many adults find reading hard
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: In the most recent international survey of adult skills, about a quarter of adults across participating countries scored at the lowest levels of literacy.
- Sources: OECD 2024

### Plain language works for experts too
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: In a survey on legal writing, preference for plain wording was strong and grew with education. In experiments, text made needlessly complex led readers to rate its author as less intelligent.
- Sources: Trudeau 2012; Oppenheimer 2006

### Readability scores are a rough check, not a target
- Grade: Contested
- Finding: Readability formulas count word and sentence length. They ignore meaning, order, layout and the reader's knowledge, and text can be rewritten to score well while becoming harder to use.
- Sources: Redish 2000

### Say it positively and directly
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: People take longer to understand negative statements than affirmative ones. Double negatives were not tested in these studies, but each adds another reversal to work out.
- Sources: Wason 1961; Clark & Chase 1972

### Headings that say what follows
- Grade: Moderate, studies with schoolchildren
- Finding: Across a series of experiments with schoolchildren, headings helped them search for and retrieve information from text, and helped older pupils recall it.
- Sources: Hartley & Trueman 1985

### Capital letters
- Grade: Moderate, old print studies
- Finding: Continuous text set in all capitals was read more slowly than lower case.
- Sources: Tinker & Paterson 1928

## 2. Naming things

### People do not agree on names
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: When people were asked to name the same object or action, any two of them chose the same word less than 20% of the time.
- Sources: Furnas et al. 1987

### One name for one thing
- Grade: Framework

## 3. Guiding

### One question at a time
- Grade: Strong, from survey research
- Finding: Survey methodology shows that people must interpret a question before they can answer it, and that ambiguous, complex or vague wording produces unreliable answers.
- Sources: Tourangeau, Rips & Rasinski 2000

## 4. Responding

### Errors: what happened and what to do
- Grade: Framework with early empirical basis
- Finding: Early experiments found that more specific and constructive system messages improved error correction and were preferred over terse or cryptic ones.
- Sources: Shneiderman 1982

### People respond to products as they do to people
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: In experiments, people applied social rules such as politeness to computers, even while knowing they were machines.
- Sources: Nass, Steuer & Tauber 1994

### Confirmations that close the loop

## 5. Including everyone

### Masculine words used as generic are not read as generic
- Grade: Strong
- Finding: "he" and masculine job titles used to mean anyone lead readers to picture men. Singular "they" is read as easily as "he" or "she" when the person is unspecified.
- Sources: Gastil 1990; Stahlberg et al. 2007; Foertsch & Gernsbacher 1997

### Wording changes who feels invited
- Grade: Moderate
- Finding: Job advertisements written with more stereotypically masculine wording were less appealing to women, who expected to belong less, though the wording did not change how skilled they felt for the job.
- Sources: Gaucher, Friesen & Kay 2011

### Disability language
- Grade: Contested
- Finding: There is no single correct form. Some people and communities prefer person-first wording ("person with epilepsy"), others identity-first ("autistic person", "Deaf person"); in one large UK survey autistic adults and professionals preferred different terms.
- Sources: Dunn & Andrews 2015; Kenny et al. 2016

## 6. Numbers and other languages

### Counts of cases are understood better than probabilities
- Grade: Strong for reasoning problems
- Finding: People solved probability problems far more often when the numbers were given as counts of cases (10 of 1,000 people have the condition, and 8 of those 10 test positive) than as probabilities or percentages.
- Sources: Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995
